Mechanism 1

The ID spine: why one number has to survive the whole journey

A trade ERP is not really a workflow tool — it is an identity tool. One master Transaction ID is created the moment the contract is signed, and every record created afterwards inherits it. That inheritance is what lets a buyer complaint in Dubai be traced back to a specific tree block in Kotkhai in two clicks, and what lets Finance attribute a ₹40,000 claim to the exact stage that caused it.

The shape of the process

Five phases, sixteen stages

The sixteen stages are not a flat list. They group into five phases, and each phase has a different owner, a different failure mode, and a different kind of risk. Getting this grouping right in the ERP matters because permissions, dashboards and escalation paths follow phases, not stages.

Handoffs

7 owner changes

Trade Desk → Procurement → QC → Transport → Warehouse → Cold Chain → Freight → CHA → Finance. Every arrow between two different owners is where work is dropped. Your ERP earns its keep at the handoff, not inside the task.

Hard deadlines

3 immovable cut-offs

Terminal gate-in cut-off, VGM submission cut-off, and shipping-instruction cut-off. Miss any one and the container rolls to the next sailing — typically seven days, on cargo with a finite shelf life.

Points of no return

2 irreversible gates

Container sealing (stage 10) and vessel departure (stage 14). After the seal number is recorded you cannot correct the contents; after departure you cannot correct anything at all except paperwork and expectations.

Stage by stage

What actually happens, who does it, and what it produces

Each stage is described the same way on purpose: trigger → system action → human action → documents → exceptions. If you build every stage of your ERP to that same shape, you get one screen template instead of sixteen, and one exception engine instead of sixteen inboxes.

Exception handling

Five scenarios, applied to every stage

Every stage can end in one of five ways. Your ERP does not need sixteen exception workflows — it needs five chains, parameterised by stage. Pick a scenario below and read down: the chain is the same shape everywhere, only the trigger and the recovery action change. This is the single biggest simplification available to you when you build.

Mechanism 4

Three clocks run at the same time — and they are not the same length

General cargo has one clock: the sailing schedule. Perishable cargo has three, and they compete. The cut-off clock is the shortest and hardest. The shelf-life clock started before you took delivery and cannot be paused, only slowed. The payment clock is the longest and is what actually consumes your working capital. A control tower that only shows the shipment clock will let you win the sailing and lose the trade.

Apple-specific rule

Apples are ethylene producers

Apples emit high levels of ethylene, which ripens and destroys ethylene-sensitive cargo. Never co-load with kiwi, leafy greens, broccoli or cut flowers. Your ERP needs a compatibility check on the stuffing plan, not just a volume check.

Apple-specific rule

Pre-cool before you load, not after

A reefer container is designed to hold temperature, not pull it down. Loading warm fruit means the set point is never truly reached in the core of the pallet. Record pulp temperature at stuffing — not air temperature — and make it a blocking field.

Apple-specific rule

Bruising is invisible for 48 hours

Impact damage at loading shows as brown discolouration days later — often first seen by the buyer. This is why stage 08 photo evidence and stage 10 pallet-scan reconciliation exist: they are the only defence when a claim arrives.

Build notes

What this process means for the data model

The process above reduces to a fairly small number of entities. Almost everything else in the ERP is a view over them. The trap is modelling stages as tables; model things as tables and stages as states, or you will end up with sixteen half-duplicated schemas that cannot answer "where is my fruit".

Core entity

Trade

One per contract. Holds the Transaction ID, buyer, incoterm, payment terms, contracted quantity and price, and the commercial documents. Everything else points at it.

States

DRAFT CONTRACTED IN_EXECUTION SHIPPED DELIVERED SETTLED LOST
Core entity

Lot

The traceability atom. One per accepted harvest batch from one farm block. Carries farmer, GPS, harvest datetime, grade, quantity, QC record and the shelf-life clock start. A trade can have many lots; a lot can serve more than one trade only if you split it — and a split must create child lots, never edit the parent.

States

CREATED IN_TRANSIT RECEIVED IN_COLD PACKED ALLOCATED SHIPPED QUARANTINE REJECTED
Core entity

Pallet

The unit that is actually scanned and loaded. Links Lot → Container. Carries carton count, gross/net weight, pulp temperature at pack, and export-QC result. This is the level at which a partial rejection happens, so it must be independently rejectable.

States

PACKED QC_PASS QC_REJECT STAGED LOADED
Core entity

Container / Shipment

Container number, seal, type, set point, ventilation, PTI result, VGM, booking, vessel, voyage, ETD/ETA, and the cut-off datetimes. The cut-off fields are the ones that drive every urgent notification in the system.

States

PLANNED BOOKED AT_WAREHOUSE STUFFED SEALED CLEARED GATED_IN ON_VESSEL DISCHARGED DELIVERED ROLLED
Core entity

Document

One polymorphic table, not one table per document type. Columns: type, owning entity (trade / lot / container), mandatory flag, issuer, issue date, expiry date, status, file, verified-by, verified-at. The D-7 / D-3 / D-1 / OVERDUE engine reads only this table.

States

NOT_APPLICABLE PENDING SUBMITTED VERIFIED REJECTED EXPIRED
Core entity

Event / Exception

Append-only. Every stage transition, every sensor excursion, every SLA breach and every scenario chain step writes one row: entity, stage, scenario type, severity, owner, opened-at, resolved-at, resolution. Your audit trail, your risk score and your post-mortem all come from this one table.

Never

Never update or delete a row here. Correction is a new row that references the old one.

Five rules that will save you a rewrite

1 · Quantity is never a single number

Contracted, harvested, QC-accepted, received, packed, loaded and delivered quantities all differ, legitimately. Store all seven. The gap between any two is a business fact someone needs to explain.

2 · Time is always two fields

Planned and actual, for every milestone. A control tower is just the delta between those two columns, rendered. Without the planned value there is no such thing as "delayed".

3 · Temperature is a stream, not an attribute

A separate time-series store keyed by lot and container, with the set point stored alongside so an excursion is computable rather than judged. Keep it for the full claim window — typically a year.

4 · Every status change needs an actor

Who, when, on what evidence. On a quality claim, "the system says PASS" is worth nothing; "inspector R. Thakur signed off at 09:14 with four photos and a GPS fix" is worth the claim amount.

5 · Costs accrue per stage

Procurement, transport, cold storage, packing, documentation, CHA, port, freight, insurance, finance cost and commission all attach to the trade with a stage tag. Stage 16 margin analysis is then a query, not a spreadsheet.

6 · The notification engine is one component

Four severities (INFO / WARNING / ACTION REQUIRED / CRITICAL), one rule table, one delivery service. Do not scatter alert logic across sixteen modules — you will never be able to answer "why did nobody get told".

Read this before you scope

What the sixteen-stage model does not yet cover

The stage model is sound. These are the things that sit outside it and will surface during build — mostly Indian export compliance that is company-level rather than trade-level, and the money that comes back after the trade is nominally closed. Confirm current requirements with your CHA and your bank; the systems and thresholds change.

01 Company-level registrations are master data, not documents

IEC, AD Code registered at each port you ship from, APEDA registration for fresh horticulture, GST LUT for zero-rated export without paying IGST, and FSSAI where required. These are not per-trade documents — they are validity-dated master records, and an expired one blocks every trade at once. Model them as a separate register with its own expiry alerts.

02 The money loop does not end at stage 16

Export proceeds have to be realised and evidenced against the shipping bill within the period your bank and FEMA require. Add an e-BRC / realisation step after settlement, and reconcile shipping bill ↔ invoice ↔ inward remittance. An unreconciled shipping bill is a compliance problem long after everyone has forgotten the trade.

03 Export incentives are margin you are not tracking

Duty drawback and RoDTEP-type scrip accrue per shipping bill and land weeks later. If they are not attached to the trade, stage 16 profitability is understated and your pricing model drifts. Treat them as a receivable opened at stage 12 and closed on credit.

04 Preferential origin changes the buyer's price

For UAE, a preferential certificate of origin under the India–UAE trade agreement can materially reduce the buyer's landed duty. That is a commercial lever your Trade Desk should be able to see at stage 01, and a different document to raise at stage 11. The mind map has only a generic "Certificate of Origin".

05 The cold chain has a blind gap on the road leg

Between stuffing (10) and gate-in (13) the reefer runs on a diesel genset on a trailer, and between gate-in and vessel loading it is plugged into terminal power — or not. Plug-out and plug-in times are rarely captured, and that is precisely where unexplained excursions occur. Add them as mandatory timestamps.

06 Rejected fruit has a second life and a value

Stage 03, 07 and 08 rejections currently just reduce quantity. In reality rejected apples go to the domestic market, to juice or processing, or to disposal — each with a different recovery value and a different document. Without a disposition workflow, your loss numbers will be wrong and consistently too pessimistic.

07 A claim is not the end of the story — recovery is

Stage 15 captures the buyer's claim. It does not capture the recovery chain: notice to the carrier within the time bar, survey report, insurance claim, and settlement against the policy. A perishable claim that is not notified in time simply becomes your loss. Model claim and recovery as two linked records.

08 Nothing yet models "who was supposed to be told"

Every scenario chain ends with a notification, but the model has no escalation matrix: owner → manager → COO, with time thresholds per severity. Build the matrix as configuration rather than code, because it will change every quarter as the team grows.
